Greg Brockman on the Founding of OpenAI and the 72 Hours That Nearly Ended It

OpenAI cofounder Greg Brockman tells Shane Parrish how the company started at a 2015 Napa offsite, narrates the 72 hours of Sam Altman’s firing from the inside, and lays out where compute, AI-written code, reasoning models, and jobs go next. Enriched transcript at the bottom.

In a Knowledge Project interview with Shane Parrish, OpenAI cofounder and president Greg Brockman walks through the company’s first decade: the 2015 dinner with Sam Altman, the Napa offsite that fixed OpenAI’s technical plan, the 72 hours in November 2023 when Altman was fired and the company nearly came apart, and where he thinks compute, jobs, and AGI (artificial general intelligence) go next. The conversation, in brief:

  • Meeting Sam Altman: how a 2015 dinner and a late-night drive turned into the decision to start OpenAI, against a consensus that DeepMind’s lead was insurmountable.
  • The founding plan still holds: the three-step bet sketched at the Napa offsite (solve reinforcement learning, then unsupervised learning, then progressively harder tasks) is the one OpenAI has run for a decade.
  • Why the nonprofit had to change: once they ran the compute math in 2017, nonprofit fundraising had a ceiling, and a for-profit arm became the only path to an AGI-scale budget.
  • The breakthroughs that felt real: the 2017 sentiment-neuron result and then Dota 2 convinced him that simple algorithms plus massive compute could reach human-level play in messy environments.
  • Prediction as a route to intelligence: Greg argues pretraining and reasoning run on the same machinery, predicting the next token on differently structured data.
  • The firing, hour by hour: where he was when the board called, the message he got, and why he quit that night.
  • He still won’t say why: Greg narrates the 72 hours in close detail but never names what actually drove the board’s decision.
  • The petition that crashed Google Docs: nearly the whole company signed, and Ilya’s overnight reversal was the moment he felt it could be put back together.
  • What he took from it: be decisive, have the hard conversation, and, in Ilya’s framing, accept that building something real means suffering.
  • AI building AI: code is “essentially all AI now,” with humans still ahead on structure and interfaces, and models starting to propose their own research ideas.
  • On sycophancy: models did start telling users what they wanted to hear, so OpenAI retrained against “hacking the grader” toward users’ long-term goals.
  • Why the reasoning traces went dark: distillation defense, plus keeping the chain-of-thought faithful instead of trained to look good.
  • A compute-constrained world: a personal AGI for everyone would need on the order of billions of GPUs, far past what’s being built, so who gets served becomes a real question.
  • The data-center bet: the spending OpenAI was teased for is, in his telling, what delivers the mission, and he entertains data centers in space.
  • The global race and regulation: why he thinks US leadership in AI matters, and where he would put guardrails (compute access, privacy and privilege, data-center water and power).
  • Jobs and what you gain: some institutions and roles won’t be as stable, but he frames the upside as agency, down to “a doctor in their pocket” for everyone.
  • Success: “ensuring that artificial general intelligence benefits all humanity.”
