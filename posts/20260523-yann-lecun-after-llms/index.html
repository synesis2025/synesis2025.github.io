Yann LeCun on What Comes After LLMs

Yann LeCun lays out his contrarian thesis on Unsupervised Learning: LLMs are useful products but a dead end on the path to human-level intelligence — they can’t predict consequences, can’t plan, and only work where language is the substrate of reasoning. JEPA, world models, Tapestry, why he left Meta, and why he had zero technical influence on Llama.

In the May 15, 2026 episode of Jacob Effron’s Unsupervised Learning podcast, Yann LeCun — Turing Award winner and former Chief AI Scientist at Meta — lays out the technical thesis behind his new company, AMI Labs, and his reasons for leaving Meta after twelve years of building FAIR. The 1h 21m conversation moves from his core claim that LLMs are not a path to human-level intelligence, through the JEPA [1] architecture he is scaling, his sovereign-AI project Tapestry, why he diverged from Geoffrey Hinton and Yoshua Bengio starting in 2023, and a remarkably candid account of his actual role at Meta — which, contrary to the public narrative, included zero technical contribution to Llama.

The main points, each linking to the supporting passage in the transcript:

  • LLMs aren’t a path to human-level intelligence — they can’t predict the consequences of their actions, can’t plan by search, and only work where language is the substrate of reasoning (math, code, problem-solving with a verifier). Useful products; not a research path.
  • JEPA learns abstract representations rather than predicting pixels. Five years ago LeCun realized every successful image/video representation method was non-generative; that crystallized into the joint-embedding predictive architecture.
  • Most “world model” robotics work today is imitation learning at huge data cost. A 17-year-old learns to drive in 20 hours; we have millions of hours of driving data and still no level-five autonomy. The bottleneck is data efficiency, not scale.
  • Tapestry is a federated training platform for sovereign open-source AI: contributors keep their data local, exchange only parameter vectors, converge to a global consensus model that countries can then fine-tune for local language, culture, and politics.
  • OpenAI is the next Sun Microsystems — closed proprietary models will get commodified by open ones the way Linux ate Solaris and HP-UX. Internet infrastructure has a natural pull toward open, and AI is becoming infrastructure.
  • LLMs are intrinsically unsafe — not because of doomer scenarios, but because they cannot predict the consequences of their actions, cannot guarantee task completion, and there is always a prompt that gets them to do something stupid. Objective-driven AI with explicit world models can be safe by construction.
  • Yann had zero technical contribution to Llama, positive or negative. His one Llama-related action was arguing internally for open-sourcing Llama 2 against the legal and policy departments. The popular narrative that he “ran” Meta’s LLM work is wrong.
  • Don’t work on LLMs for your PhD — academia can’t compete on GPUs, and explaining why LLMs work is descriptive science. Work on the next generation. LeCun’s current bet: SIGReg [17] (Sketched Isotropic Gaussian Regularization) and other information-maximization regularizers — descendants of Becker & Hinton’s 1992 mutual-information objective [13] — to prevent representation collapse in JEPA, with LeWorldModel [19] as the latest demonstration.

Defining a world model: predicting consequences of actions

Okay. Then there is world models. So what is a world model? A world model at a very general level is something that allows an agentic system to anticipate the consequences of its own actions — predict the consequences of its own actions.

From my point of view, I cannot imagine how you can even think of building an agentic system without that system having the ability to predict the consequences of its actions. I mean, that’s pretty essential, right?

When we act in the world, we have this ability, and when we take an action without thinking about the consequences, we are taking a big risk. And very often, you know, other people think we’re an idiot.

We have plenty of examples on the international political scene at the moment of people who have complete, you know, ability to predict the consequences of their actions.

So the blueprint for this is completely different from what LLMs can do at the moment. LLMs do not have the ability to predict the consequences of their actions, and they do not have any planning abilities, because inference is by predicting the next token, right? It’s not by search.

Cognitive science System 1 vs. System 2 as motivation

Jacob Effron: — level of representation. So as you were working on this architecture, was a lot of it inspired by the human brain? I mean, obviously the way you’re articulating things — exactly how we do things.

Yann LeCun: Right, or at least by, you know, cognitive science. Whether you can sort of translate this into a neural architecture and things like this — there’s a big gap there.

Okay, so certainly cognitive science was a bit of a motivation, or what psychologists call System 2, which is this idea that the way you behave in sort of deliberate, reflective behavior is that you do imagine, predict the consequences of your actions, and you plan accordingly — contrary to System 1 where you just act reactively and instinctively.

So yeah, there is an inspiration. But also there is a lot of empirical evidence that you don’t want to generate pixels.

Five-year-old epiphany: every successful representation method is non-generative

Okay, I’ve been really interested in that problem of learning models of the world by prediction for a very long time, and then had an epiphany about five years ago realizing that all of the architectures that have been successful to learn representations of images and videos are non-generative architectures, and all the generative ones basically have been failures.

Right? So VAEs [2] — variational autoencoders — or autoencoders more generally. It’s kind of a natural way to think about learning abstract representations of inputs. So you put an image at the input of a neural net and then you train it to just reproduce the input on its output.

Now, with a big neural net — if you just do it this way, your neural net will not do anything interesting. You just learn the identity function.

Jacob Effron: Yeah.

Yann LeCun: Completely uninteresting. It doesn’t work. Like if you train a VAE to learn representations of images, you get something, but it’s really not that great. Same with sparse autoencoders.

Denoising and masked autoencoders: another disappointment

Then you have another set of techniques, and it’s kind of a derivative of something called the denoising autoencoder. Masked autoencoder [3] is a version of this. BERT [4] is a version of this for NLP.

So you take the image, you corrupt it in some way, and then you train this big neural net to recover the original image. There’s a huge project at FAIR on this called MAE — masked autoencoder. It was very disappointing. A lot of computation, and not really great satisfying results.

JEPA: joint-embedding predictive architecture

Simultaneously, some of the same people working on MAE and some other people in Paris and in New York were working on other techniques using non-generative architecture — joint embedding architecture. So take an image, corrupt it in some way, and then run the two images through encoders, and then try to predict the representation of the original image from the representation of the corrupted one. That’s JEPA [1].

Jacob Effron: Yeah.

Yann LeCun: Okay. So JEPA means joint-embedding predictive architecture, right? So you have one encoder that observes — makes an observation — another encoder that makes a different observation. You try to predict the representation of the first one from the second one with a predictor.

And those techniques turned out to work much better for representing images and video. So things like DINO [5], DINO V1, V2, V3 [6] — projects that are still going on at FAIR in Paris. Projects like I-JEPA [1], and then V-JEPA [7], and then before that there were like SimCLR [8] and MoCo [9], and like a whole bunch of different techniques, mostly from Meta. There was a bunch of others from other groups.

But that turned out to be a much better way of learning representations of images than predicting pixels.

Jacob Effron: Yeah.

Yann LeCun: And so it just clicked in my mind, but, you know, not just mine, that this was the way to go and predicting pixels was kind of a losing proposition.

Jacob Effron: You know, it feels like there’s all these robotics demos that are released from some of the model companies that feel increasingly impressive and maybe seem to resemble things like planning and reasoning when, you know, they maybe haven’t seen a room or a specific version of a task before, and are still able to execute that task.

What would you say to our listeners that observe that stuff and feel like, ah, it feels like we’re trending toward some real progress with some of the generative approaches?

Yann LeCun: Well, there is real progress, and some of those demos are really impressive. But they are trained with enormous amounts of data, collected either from teleoperation or from just human action with things you hold in your hand that look like —

Jacob Effron: Grippers.

Yann LeCun: Grippers, that you collect the data for. Or just tracking hands and fingers of a person, and then translating this into commands for a robot. And so those things are trained with imitation learning mostly, and a little bit with reinforcement learning to fine-tune, mostly in simulation.

So the issue with this is that you need a lot of data to train those systems with imitation, and it becomes expensive. And it’s a little brittle, in the sense that you need to collect lots of data for every task you want the robot to solve.

Whereas if the system had a world model that allowed it to predict the outcome of an action, it would just plan an action to solve a new task without actually having to be trained to accomplish this task. So the degree of generalization you would get with a world-model-based system is much, much larger — you know, kind of wider spectrum of tasks with less training data than would be required by a system trained with imitation learning and fine-tuning.

Zero-shot generalization, and the 17-year-old driver

Yann LeCun: I mean, you get this synergy between tasks. So the more tasks that you train the system to solve, the more tasks it’s going to be able to acquire with a small amount of data, regardless of what technique you use.

But the hope with world models is that the system can solve much zero-shot, which humans are completely capable of doing, right? And many animals as well. So that’s really the hope — solving a lot more problems with either a small amount of training data, or no training data at all, and just a little bit of maybe RL-style fine-tuning.

Yeah. Like, how is it that a 17-year-old can learn to drive in like a dozen hours, or maybe 20 hours? We have millions of hours of training data of people driving cars. I feel like there’s this dream of using video models to just generate tons of synthetic data for simulation. And, you know, even if it’s not perfect, these video models from a physics perspective are like helpful enough to improve robotics and the underlying physical world.

And it’s the problem that, if you take two inputs — let’s say the initial segment of a video and the continuation of that video, or you take one image and a corrupted version of it — you run them both through an encoder, and you train a predictor to predict the representation of one from the representation of the other, there’s a very simple solution where the system basically predicts a constant representation.

Note

Editor’s note. A useful intuition for why this is JEPA’s defining problem: the JEPA training signal measures agreement between two encoded representations, not their content. Concretely, take two related inputs (e.g., a video frame \(x_t\) and a future frame \(x_{t+k}\)). Pass each through the encoder: \(z_t = E(x_t)\), \(z_{t+k} = E(x_{t+k})\). Train a predictor \(P\) to map \(z_t \to \hat{z}_{t+k}\). The loss is \(\| P(z_t) - z_{t+k} \|^2\).

Now suppose the encoder degenerates so that \(E(x) = c\) — the same constant vector — for every input. Then \(z_t = c\) and \(z_{t+k} = c\) for any pair of frames. The predictor is only ever queried at one input, \(c\), so its output is some fixed vector \(p = P(c)\) — whatever it happens to be. Gradient descent then jointly drives \(c\) and \(p\) to coincide, and the loss \(\|p - c\|^2\) reaches zero. Perfect minimization, zero learning. The two representations agree because the entire encoder-predictor pair has collapsed to a trivial fixed point, not because the model has discovered anything about the inputs.

Generative models (autoregressive LLMs, VAEs, diffusion) don’t have this failure mode because they have to reconstruct the input from the representation, which anchors it — a constant encoder would make every input’s reconstruction the same, blowing up the loss. JEPA has no reconstruction term; only the consistency signal, which the trivial solution kills perfectly.

Concretely, a collapsed encoder is downstream-useless — every input maps to \(c\), so the model can’t distinguish anything. The three fix families LeCun describes next each kill the trivial fixed point in a different way: contrastive methods force pairs of different inputs apart with explicit negative samples; distillation/EMA methods (BYOL [15], DINO [5]) break the symmetric gradient path that would otherwise drive both encoders to a constant; and information-content regularizers (VICReg [18], SIGReg [17]) directly penalize low-entropy representations.

So the big question of self-supervised learning for JEPA, for the joint-embedding architecture, is: how do you prevent collapse?

Note

Editor’s note. The intuition behind the regularizer family: collapse is the limit case where the encoder outputs carry zero information about the input — every input maps to the same \(c\), so the entropy of the representation distribution is zero. If you can force the encoder’s output distribution to have high entropy — i.e., spread across the latent space rather than concentrated on a single point — collapse is mechanically excluded. No need for negative samples (contrastive) or asymmetric architectural tricks (BYOL/DINO); the regularizer term in the loss does the work directly.

The technical obstacle LeCun flags is that differential entropy of a continuous distribution isn’t easy to estimate from samples — you can only construct upper bounds (estimators that overstate true entropy). Maximizing an upper bound isn’t a guarantee, so practitioners maximize a tractable proxy and hope the bound is tight enough.

The two methods named here implement that proxy differently:

  • VICReg [18] is three terms — Variance (each feature dimension must have at least unit variance, so no dimension can collapse), Invariance (paired views agree on their representations, the original JEPA consistency signal), and Covariance (off-diagonal terms of the feature covariance matrix are pushed to zero, decorrelating features so they don’t all encode the same information). Spread + decorrelate.
  • SIGReg [17] is one term: project the encoder output onto many random 1-D directions and run a normality (Epps–Pulley) test on each projection. Aggregating the test statistics pushes the joint distribution toward an isotropic Gaussian. Why an isotropic Gaussian? Under fixed variance, the Gaussian distribution has maximum differential entropy — so matching that target is equivalent to maximizing information content under a unit-variance constraint. The LeJEPA paper [17] proves the isotropic Gaussian is the optimal target distribution for JEPAs.

So this is super promising, in my opinion. And we have variations of it — one that can produce sparse representations, another one that can produce isotropic representations but not necessarily Gaussians.

So if you want to read one paper, read that paper. It’s LeWorldModel [19].

Note

Editor’s note. When LeCun says “a paper with Randall — student at Mila,” he conflates two people. Randall Balestriero is the assistant prof at Brown; the student at Mila is Lucas Maes, lead author of LeWorldModel [19].
